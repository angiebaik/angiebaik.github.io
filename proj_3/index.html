Project 3 Part A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

I used the pretrained flow model PixNerd (512 x 512 images) to implement sampling, classifier-free guidance, image editing, visual anagrams, and hybrid images. I did not have to do any model training for Part A. Throughout, time t = 0 is pure noise and t = 1 is a clean image, the Euler step size is h = 0.02, and prompts are turned into embeddings with model.encode. My random seed is 180, excdpt for certain sections like visual anagrams and hybrid images in order to create a better image generation.

Part 1: Visualizing Training and Sampling

1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

The noisy input is a linear interpolation between Gaussian noise \(x_0 = \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and the clean image \(x_1\): \[ x_t = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad t \in [0, 1]. \] This is not just adding noise to the image, as noise is also being scaled by \(t\). I checked t = 0 returning noise and t = 1 returning the clean image, so I could just reuse one noise tensor.

My image is a photo from Seattle at Kerry Park, showcasing the Seattle skyline with Mount Rainer in the background. My original image followed by the noisy versions at t = 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75:

Original, t = 0.25, t = 0.50, t = 0.75

1.2 Classical Denoising

As a baseline I restored the image amplitude by dividing by t, since \[ \frac{x_t}{t} = x_1 + \frac{1 - t}{t}\,\epsilon, \] and then applied a Gaussian blur. I chose the sigmas for the gaussian blur with sigma = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and kept the sigma with the lowest mean squared error against the clean image. Each image shows the noisy input next to its best blurred version.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

The pretrained model predicts a velocity, not noise. I extrapolate from \(x_t\) to the clean endpoint with one prediction, using the embedding \(c\) for "a high quality photo": \[ \hat{x}_1 = x_t + (1 - t)\, v_\theta(x_t, t, c). \]

1.4 Visualizing Velocity

The update the model adds in 1.3 is (1 - t) * v̂. Since I know the clean image, the ground-truth update is \((1 - t)\,v_{gt} = x_1 - x_t\), where \(v_{gt} = x_1 - \epsilon\). The error map is the per-pixel RMS difference, averaged over the RGB channels: \[ E_t = \sqrt{\operatorname{mean}_{\text{RGB}}\!\left[\big((1 - t)(\hat{v} - v_{gt})\big)^2\right]}. \]

1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

One-step denoising is inaccurate because the learned velocity changes along the path. Instead I take small Euler steps, \(x_{t+h} = x_t + h\, v(x_t, t)\), and recompute the velocity after every update. I used h = 0.02 and shortened the last step when needed so the sampler ends exactly at t = 1. Starting from the saved t = 0.50 noisy image, that is 25 Euler updates.

I tested it on a constant velocity field, where the answer is known exactly, starting at t = 0 (meaning we have 50 full steps), t = 0.5 (25 steps), and t = 0.93 (a shortened final step).

Part 2: Conditional Generation and Guidance

2.1 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

Classifier-free guidance evaluates the model twice at the same x_t and t. One evaluation is with the prompt (v_c) and once with the empty-string embedding (v_u). The guided velocity is \[ v_{cfg} = v_c + w\,(v_c - v_u), \] with \(w = 7\), so \(w = 0\) is ordinary conditional sampling. I checked that w = 0 reproduces the conditional prediction.

Part 3: Creative Applications

Throughout this section I use h = 0.02, w = 7, and the empty prompt for the unconditional prediction.

3.1 Image-to-Image Editing

Following SDEdit, I add noise to a real image up to a starting time t_start with the forward process, then run the CFG Euler sampler from t_start to 1 with "a high quality photo". A smaller starting time means more noise and a bigger edit, and a larger one keeps more of the original. I used starting times [0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.10, 0.14, 0.25, 0.5] and the same noise tensor (seed 180) for every image and starting time.

3.3 Visual Anagrams

A visual anagram looks like one thing upright and another when rotated 180 degrees. At every Euler step I compute the CFG velocity of the image with prompt A, rotate the image, compute the CFG velocity with prompt B, rotate that velocity back, and average the two: \[ v_1 = v_{cfg}(x_t, t, p_1), \qquad v_2 = F\big(v_{cfg}(F(x_t), t, p_2)\big), \qquad v = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2}, \] where \(F\) rotates by 180 degrees. One thing to note is that I looped through 6 different seeds in order to find a good output, and the seeds are listed with the image.

3.4 Hybrid Images

Adapting Factorized Diffusion, I predict two CFG velocities at the same x_t and t, and keep the low frequencies of prompt A and the high frequencies of prompt B. \[ v = f_{\text{lowpass}}(v_a) + f_{\text{highpass}}(v_b), \qquad f_{\text{highpass}}(v) = v - f_{\text{lowpass}}(v). \] The low-pass is a Gaussian blur with kernel size 265 and sigma 16 at 512 x 512. Prompt A is the distant, low-frequency subject and prompt B is the close-up, high-frequency subject. The composite velocity is used in the Euler update. I also ran through 6 different seeds to find the best output for the image.

Each hybrid is shown at full size (the close-up subject should dominate) and reduced to 64 x 64 (the distant subject should appear).

Most Important Thing I Learned

The most important thing I learned is that one sampler can do many different jobs. The Euler loop never changes. It takes a small step along the predicted velocity, then asks the model again. I also learned how greatly images can change based on a singular seed change. Also Classifier-free guidance made a big difference in quality too, since pushing the prediction away from the unconditional one made the samples look much more like real photos.
