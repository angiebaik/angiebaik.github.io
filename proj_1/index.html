Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Introduction

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii took photos of the Russian Empire using a technique that recorded three exposures (red, green and blue filters) onto a glass plate. There are digitized copes in greyscale. This project reconstructs color by splitting the plate into the three channels. Then it aligns the G and R channels to the B channel with translations, and stacking them into a singular RGB image.

How Does The Program Do It?

My implementation takes in the glass plate image and then outputs a coor image. First I separate the image into 3 equal parts by its height for the different channels (R, G, B). Then I take the three images and stack them on top of each other by aligning the G and R channel to the B channel photo. In order to align the images I would search through a window a certain size and scored the alignment of the photos based on certain metrics like L2 or NCC. Before scoring I would crop out a certain cutoff percentage based on the image size (5%), so that the wrap around pixels from np.roll do not ruin the score.

Alignment Scoring

As mentioned before there are two different metrics to score how well the alignment is. I used both Euclidean distance (L2) and Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC).

Euclidean Distance (L2)

With two image \(A\) and \(B\), I calculate the L2 score by comparing the pixel values at every location. For each pixel, I take the difference between the two images and square it. I then sum all of these squared differences and take the square root. A lower L2 score means the images are more similar, so during alignment I choose the shift with the smallest score. The Euclidean distance at each pixel was found with:

\[ d(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (u_i-v_i)^2 } \]

With two different images of equal size A, B the L2 Score is determined by:

\[ \|A-B\|_2 = \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} (A_{ij}-B_{ij})^2 } \]

Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC)

For NCC, I first subtract the mean pixel value from each image patch. This centers the pixel values around zero and makes the comparison less dependent on the overall brightness of the images. I then calculate the dot product between the two centered patches and normalize it by their magnitudes. A higher NCC score means the images are more similar, so I choose the shift with the highest score.

\[ NCC(A,B) = \frac{ \sum_{i,j}(A_{ij}-\bar{A})(B_{ij}-\bar{B}) }{ \sqrt{ \sum_{i,j}(A_{ij}-\bar{A})^2 \sum_{i,j}(B_{ij}-\bar{B})^2 } } \]

Single Scale Alignment

For the lower resolution .jpg images, I would search over a window of [-15, 15] pixel in both the x and y. Then I would score based on NCC.

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

The full-resolution .tif scans are too large to search exhaustively with the same window size, because the displacement could be as much as 100 pixels. Therefore I took a different approach. First I recursively downsized each channel by a factor of 2 with cv.resize which uses area-averaging interpolation until reach the base case of the image size being at most 150 pixels. Then at coarsest level I ran the same search mentioned before except I used L2 as the scoring metric. Then I would upscale and double the previous level's estimate and refined it with a small sesarch window of [-5, 5] instead of [-15, 15] around that estimate. This was to optimize and improve the speed of the alignment process.

Difficulties

Church

I ran into many difficulties with the church.tif file because it did not align cleanly under NCC scoring and with a set cutoff window. Therefore I changed it to a percentage based cut off number to insure that I was not cropping a large portion of pixels once the image was downsized. I also moved onto L2 scoring rather than NCC. I think it performed better due to the fact that it's simpler scoring worked better in the smaller image since it would be downsized to 150 x 150 pixels max. The image has a large sky and water below it so I think once downsized the patterns were not as apparent so using NCC may have confused the algorithm. Therefore looking at the value itself per pixel worked better when working with a small image.

Emir

I also had issues with the emir.tif photo because it was not aligning correctly with the original image, specifically the red filter. The three different color channels not having the same brightness/intensity patterns causes an issue with aligning based on intensity values. Therefore I had to add a function that would preprocess the array with Sobel filter to highlight the edges and it resolved the issue.
